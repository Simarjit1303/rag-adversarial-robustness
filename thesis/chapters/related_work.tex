\chapter{Related Work}

\section{Indirect Prompt Injection}

\citet{greshake2023not} established indirect prompt injection as a
distinct attack class, demonstrating that real-world LLM-integrated
applications could be compromised by instructions embedded in content
the model retrieves or processes, not in the user's own input. Their
work was the first to frame this as a structurally different threat from
a jailbreak, since the attacker never interacts with the system at all;
the malicious content simply waits in a document, a webpage, or an email
for the model to encounter it during normal operation. Their evaluation
demonstrates the attack qualitatively, through real-world case studies
against deployed integrations, rather than the quantified, cross-model
attack-success-rate comparison this dissertation's own methodology
builds on those foundations to produce. \citet{liu2024formalizing}
built on this by formalising prompt injection attacks and defenses into
a shared benchmarking methodology, published at USENIX Security 2024,
with explicit attack-success metrics that later work could compare
against directly rather than each paper inventing its own measurement
convention. This dissertation's own attack-success-rate methodology,
detailed in Chapter 4, follows a comparable measurement approach, applied
specifically to the RAG retrieval surface rather than to the general
LLM-integrated applications Liu et al. study, a narrower scope chosen
deliberately so that every result in this dissertation can be attributed
to the retrieval pipeline itself rather than to some other integration
point. \citet{zhan2024injecagent} extends the injection threat model to
tool-integrated LLM agents, where an injected instruction can trigger
unintended tool calls, not just an unintended text response. This is
adjacent context for the threat landscape, not something this
dissertation builds on directly, since the benchmark here targets a
plain retrieve-then-generate pipeline without agentic tool use; an
injected instruction in this dissertation's setting can only ever change
what text the model produces, never trigger an external action.

\section{Knowledge Poisoning of RAG Systems}

\citet{zou2025poisonedrag} introduced PoisonedRAG, the knowledge-poisoning
attack implemented directly in this dissertation: an adversary inserts a
small number of crafted documents into the knowledge base such that, for
a chosen query, the poisoned documents are retrieved and guide the model
toward a specific false answer. The attack's original formulation splits
each poisoned document into a retrieval component, designed to win the
similarity search, and a generation component, designed to steer the
model's answer once retrieved, a two-part structure this dissertation's
own implementation preserves. The original paper evaluates the attack in
isolation, with no defended condition and no backend-controlled
comparison; testing it against three structurally distinct defenses
under conditions that isolate an infrastructure confound is this
dissertation's own extension of that design. \citet{moradi2026defending}
proposes a 2026 defense benchmarked directly against PoisonedRAG, using
cross-encoder activation signals to detect poisoned passages before they
reach the generation stage; its continued use of PoisonedRAG as the
reference attack, more than two years after the original paper, is
evidence that the attack remains the field's active benchmark for
knowledge poisoning rather than having been superseded by something
newer. That defense also operates on internal cross-encoder activation
signals, requiring access to model internals a deployer may not have;
all three defenses tested here work externally and model-agnostically
instead, a more restrictive but more broadly deployable class of
mitigation.

\section{Multi-Turn Jailbreaking}

\citet{russinovich2025great} introduced Crescendo, the multi-turn
jailbreak attack implemented directly in this dissertation, which
escalates a benign-seeming conversation gradually across several turns
toward a harmful or policy-violating output that a model would refuse if
asked directly in a single turn. The attack's core insight is that a
model's safety training is calibrated against single-turn requests, so a
goal broken into a sequence of individually innocuous-looking steps can
slip past defenses tuned for the more obvious, one-shot version of the
same request. Russinovich et al.'s own tests target a different set of
deployed conversational systems; running the same attack against the
four open-weight models already benchmarked here against two other
attack families is what lets this dissertation compare all three attacks
head to head in Chapter 5, something a single-system evaluation was
never designed to support. The behaviour pool used to instantiate
Crescendo's escalation targets in this dissertation draws on two
established sources: \citet{chao2024jailbreakbench}'s JBB-Behaviors,
contributing 100 behaviours and 20\% of the stratified pool, and
\citet{mazeika2024harmbench}'s HarmBench, contributing 510 behaviours
and the remaining 80\%. Combining the two sources this way follows
current practice in the guardrail literature, where a single behaviour
set is generally considered too narrow to represent the diversity of
real harmful-request categories on its own. \citet{nakka2026bitbypass}
is contemporaneous 2026 work, published at EACL 2026, that jailbreaks
aligned models through bitstream camouflage rather than conversational
escalation, a fundamentally different delivery channel that this
dissertation's Crescendo implementation does not test and does not
claim to cover.

\section{Defenses}

\citet{hines2024defending} introduced spotlighting, the defense
implemented directly in this dissertation, in its encoding-mode variant,
which the original paper reports as the strongest configuration against
indirect prompt injection, closer to a 0\% attack success rate than the
paper's alternative datamarking variant achieves. The original
spotlighting evaluation covers injection alone, outside a retrieval
pipeline; testing it under genuine retrieval noise and against a second,
structurally distinct attack family is where this dissertation's own
severe utility-cost finding for spotlighting first surfaces, a result
their evaluation had no occasion to reveal. For the
instruction-detection defense, this dissertation uses a classifier
fine-tuned from the DeBERTaV3 architecture \citep{he2023debertav3},
published at ICLR 2023, specifically \citet{protectai2024deberta}'s
released \texttt{deberta-v3-base-prompt-injection-v2} model. The closest
peer-reviewed comparator for this style of defense is
\citet{liu2025datasentinel}'s DataSentinel, published at IEEE Symposium
on Security and Privacy 2025, a game-theoretic detection method that
models the interaction between attacker and defender explicitly rather
than training a static classifier; a different, pre-trained classifier
was used in this dissertation in practice, for the practical reason that
fine-tuning a comparable detector from scratch was outside this
project's compute and time budget. The two approaches trade off
differently: DataSentinel's game-theoretic training against a simulated
attacker offers stronger in-principle robustness at the cost of a
training pipeline most labs do not already have in place, while reusing
an existing pretrained classifier sacrifices some of that robustness for
a setup any researcher can reproduce without retraining anything. The
output-filter defense uses \citet{metaai2025llamaguard4}'s Llama Guard
4, cited here as a released model rather than a peer-reviewed study.
Finally, \citet{cui2025orbench}'s OR-Bench, from ICML 2025, and
\citet{rottger2024xstest}'s XSTest, from NAACL 2024, provide the
over-refusal and false-positive benchmarking used to evaluate whether
these defenses reject legitimate, benign queries, a concern distinct
from, but as important as, their effectiveness against genuine attacks.
XSTest's 450 prompts, 250 marked safe and 200 marked unsafe, are paired
in this dissertation with OR-Bench's harder-but-still-safe prompt set
specifically because XSTest's own safe-prompt coverage is comparatively
narrow; the two together give a broader picture of whether a defense
over-triggers on ordinary, non-malicious input than either benchmark
provides alone.

\section{Reference Implementations Consulted}
Beyond the published papers discussed above, this dissertation's own
attack implementations are original code, not adaptations of an
existing codebase. A repository-wide search of the source tree and its
full commit history found no verbatim external code, no vendored files,
and no third-party license headers anywhere in the attack modules. This
dissertation nonetheless consulted two external implementations during
development, not as a source of code to copy, but to check
methodological fidelity against each paper's own description of its
algorithm.\footnote{Neither repository serves as a source of copied
code; this dissertation discloses both as implementations it read for
methodological correctness while reproducing each paper's described
algorithm, consistent with standard practice for reimplementing a
published attack.} For PoisonedRAG, the paper's own official reference
implementation, \texttt{sleeepeer/PoisonedRAG} on GitHub, informed this
dissertation's implementation of the attack's two-part poisoned-document
structure. For Crescendo, Microsoft's PyRIT toolkit provides a
\texttt{CrescendoOrchestrator} that serves as the reference
implementation of the paper's escalation-and-backtrack strategy; this
dissertation originally consulted it at its earlier repository location,
\texttt{Azure/PyRIT}, since archived and redirected to its current
canonical location, \texttt{microsoft/PyRIT}. Consulting a paper's own
reference implementation where one exists is standard practice when
reimplementing a published attack, since it reduces the risk of a
subtle misreading of the paper's prose diverging from what the original
authors actually built and evaluated.

\section{Gap This Thesis Addresses}

Two gaps in the existing literature motivate this dissertation's design,
summarized in Table~\ref{tab:gap-comparison} against the three named
comparator benchmarks introduced in Chapter 1.

First, no existing work evaluates all three attack families against the
same set of models under a single, backend-controlled harness. SafeRAG
\citep{liang2025saferag} covers a broader attack taxonomy but omits
multi-turn attacks and does not test this dissertation's four target
models. RSB \citep{zhang2025rsb} covers poisoning at far greater scale,
but exclusively, with no injection or multi-turn family in its design at
all. Hidden-in-Plain-Text \citep{guo2026hidden} covers injection
specifically but not poisoning or multi-turn jailbreaking. None of the
three, individually or together, offers what this dissertation's design
provides: the same four models, the same infrastructure, and the same
statistical methodology applied across all three attack families at
once, which is precisely what makes a genuine cross-attack comparison,
like the one presented in Chapter 5, possible in the first place. The
backend-confound discovery detailed in Chapters 4 and 5, where an
inference-backend mismatch between baseline and defended runs produced a
misleading defense result until corrected, is itself evidence that this
kind of infrastructure-level control is a methodological consideration
most of this literature does not appear to address explicitly; a
benchmark that never tests multiple defenses under a matched
infrastructure setup has no occasion to discover a confound of this
particular kind.

Second, no existing work bridges the gap between practical RAG
robustness evaluations and concrete compliance-testing requirements
under Article 15 of the EU AI Act. SafeRAG, RSB, and Hidden-in-Plain-Text
were all built as security benchmarks in their own right, and none frame
their results against a specific regulatory text. This dissertation's
mapping of its own findings onto Article 15(5)'s vulnerability classes,
developed fully in Chapters 5 and 6, is offered as a first step toward
closing that gap, rather than a claim that a finalised, harmonised
technical standard for this kind of testing yet exists; the mapping is
explicitly a technical exercise applied after the fact to results that
were designed as a security benchmark first, not a compliance-testing
tool designed from the ground up.
