\chapter{Evaluation and Results}

\section{Experimental Setup}

This chapter reports results from the benchmark design established in
Chapters 3 and 4: four target models, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
\citep{grattafiori2024llama3}, Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3},
Phi-4-mini-instruct \citep{abouelenin2025phi4mini}, and
Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512 \citep{mistralai2025ministral3}, evaluated
across two retrieval corpora, HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa} and MS
MARCO \citep{nguyen2016msmarco}, against three attack families, indirect
prompt injection \citep{greshake2023not}, PoisonedRAG
\citep{zou2025poisonedrag}, and Crescendo \citep{russinovich2025great},
and three defenses, spotlighting \citep{hines2024defending},
instruction-detection, and output-filter using Llama Guard 4
\citep{metaai2025llamaguard4}. Every generation reported in this chapter
ran on real GPU hardware rather than simulated or estimated output, and
every reported statistic uses paired testing appropriate to its
comparison type: McNemar's exact test for paired same-corpus model
comparisons, Fisher's exact test for unpaired corpus-versus-corpus
comparisons, and Holm-Bonferroni correction applied within each family
of hypotheses to control for the number of comparisons made.

Attack success rate (ASR) captures the proportion of attempts in which
the attack achieves its stated objective, whether that objective is
eliciting a specific answer, bypassing a safety filter, or causing a
refusal:
$$
\mathrm{ASR} = \frac{n_{\text{success}}}{n_{\text{total}}}
$$
where $n_{\text{success}}$ is the number of successful attack attempts
and $n_{\text{total}}$ is the total number of attack attempts. Defense
effectiveness follows from ASR directly, the change in ASR under a
defended condition relative to the matched undefended baseline. Utility
cost tracks the same logic on the answer-quality side, the change in
F1 under a defended condition relative to the clean, no-attack baseline
reported in Section~\ref{sec:baseline-quality}:
$$
\Delta F_1 = F_1^{\text{baseline}} - F_1^{\text{condition}}
$$
where $F_1^{\text{condition}}$ is the answer-quality F1 measured under
the attacked or defended condition being evaluated.

\section{Baseline Answer Quality}
\label{sec:baseline-quality}

Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-f1} reports Phase 1 baseline answer quality
(F1 and exact-match) for all four target models across all three
corpora, before any attack or defense is applied. F1 balances precision
and recall over the tokens shared between a generated answer and its
gold reference:
$$
F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}}
$$
with precision the fraction of the generated answer's tokens that
appear in the gold reference, and recall the fraction of the gold
reference's tokens that appear in the generated answer. Exact Match is
the stricter of the two, awarding credit only when the generated answer
matches the gold reference exactly after normalization. It is computed
as
$$
\mathrm{EM} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{1}\left[\hat{a}_i = a_i\right]
$$
where $\hat{a}_i$ is the model's normalized generated answer for item
$i$, $a_i$ is the normalized gold reference, and $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ is
the indicator function returning 1 when they match exactly and 0
otherwise. This baseline establishes the clean-condition reference
point every attack and defense result in this chapter is measured
against, and it is the figure any downstream utility-cost number in
this chapter implicitly compares back to.

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\texttt{nq\_open}}{nq\_open} Exclusion}

This dissertation originally intended Natural Questions Open
\citep{kwiatkowski2019natural} as a third retrieval corpus alongside
HotpotQA and MS MARCO. Figure~\ref{fig:nq-open-exclusion} reports mean
F1 of 0.962 (spread 0.005) on \texttt{nq\_open}, versus 0.585 (spread
0.179) on HotpotQA and 0.255 (spread 0.042) on MS MARCO, consistently
across all four models.

A score this close to ceiling, and this invariant across four models of
markedly different capability, is not a sign of genuine task difficulty
being solved uniformly well; it is the signature of a corpus that leaks
its own answer. The technical explanation is a corpus-construction fact,
not a model or implementation failure: no genuine supporting passage
ships with \texttt{nq\_open}, so its passage text \emph{is} the gold
answer by construction. This dissertation therefore dropped it from
every real sweep. The scope impact of this exclusion is narrower than
"losing a corpus" outright: Crescendo is corpus-independent and entirely
unaffected. Only the two corpus-dependent attack families, indirect
prompt injection and PoisonedRAG, lose one of three corpora, reducing
their design to a 4-model-by-2-corpus matrix rather than
4-by-3.\footnote{The dissertation supervisor confirmed and approved this
exclusion as a settled scope decision. The technical reasoning above,
not the approval itself, is the argument for it.} The real matrix going
forward, then, is four models by two corpora by three attacks, with
Crescendo unaffected by the corpus reduction.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig02_nq_open_exclusion_evidence}

\section{Attack 1: Indirect Prompt Injection}

This attack tests four models across two corpora against five injection
templates, organized into two categories: goal-hijacking, which redirects
the model to a different question entirely (\texttt{naive},
\texttt{escape\_char}, \texttt{combined}), and process-hijacking, which
instead convinces the model it is at a different stage of its own task
(\texttt{ignore}, \texttt{fake\_completion}). This taxonomy is a design
decision made for this benchmark rather than a direct import from
\citet{liu2024formalizing}'s original formulation. This attack places
the injection text into an organically retrieved document rather than
forcing it into the top-$k$ results, keeping this attack's surface
distinct from PoisonedRAG's forced-retrieval mechanism.
Figure~\ref{fig:injection-asr} visualizes the resulting pattern;
Table~\ref{tab:injection-full} lists every cell.

Llama-3.1-8B remains near-zero across every template and corpus.
Qwen3-8B shows elevated ASR specifically under \texttt{fake\_completion}
(11.7--14.1\%) while remaining largely unaffected by \texttt{ignore}
(0.3--3.2\%). Phi-4-mini shows elevated ASR under \texttt{fake\_completion}
on MS MARCO only, 10.1\% versus 0.2\% on HotpotQA, a corpus-specific
split confirmed statistically real ($p=8.18\times10^{-29}$).
Ministral-3-8B shows the highest ASR overall, 30.5--60.8\% under both
process-hijack templates. Across the three goal-hijack templates, ASR
sits at 0.1--1.1\% almost everywhere; utility damage there, detailed
below, is small and inconsistent in sign, every one of 24 goal-hijack
cells falling between $-0.038$ and $+0.025$.

These four patterns point to different failure modes, not a
single axis of "more or less robust." Qwen3-8B's selective vulnerability
to \texttt{fake\_completion} but not \texttt{ignore} is hypothesized,
though not proven, to relate to its explicit thinking-mode chat template
being more susceptible to a forged completion signal than a blunt
instruction override; this dissertation does not test that hypothesis
directly and reports it only as a plausible explanation, not a
confirmed mechanism. A similar but distinct explanation may apply to
Phi-4-mini's corpus-specific split: MS MARCO's shorter, less clearly
bounded passage fragments could plausibly give a forged completion
signal more room to blend in, since HotpotQA's longer passages leave
less ambiguity about where retrieved content ends and any injected text
begins, though this dissertation tests this no more directly than the
Qwen3-8B hypothesis above. Ministral-3-8B's broader vulnerability across
both process-hijack templates suggests a weaker general defense against
this attack category specifically, rather than a template-specific gap
the way Qwen3-8B and Phi-4-mini show. The concentration of utility
damage in exactly the process-hijack cells that also show elevated ASR,
and its near-absence in goal-hijack cells, supports reading utility
damage as tracking compliance directly rather than existing as a
separate, compliance-independent context-pollution effect; the one
documented exception, Phi-4-mini's small but real utility cost under
near-zero ASR, is reported below as a distinct, narrower finding rather
than folded into this general pattern.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig03_injection_asr_model_template}
\caption{Indirect injection ASR by model and template.}
\label{fig:injection-asr}
\end{figure}

Two corrections apply to every number above, both already incorporated,
and neither changes any ASR value or significance verdict, since ASR
here is a raw substring check untouched by either fix. First, the Phase
1 baseline used for utility-drop comparisons was originally inflated by
a dictionary-representation leak, the same category of
corpus-construction issue behind the \texttt{nq\_open} exclusion, before
this benchmark fixed it for both HotpotQA and MS MARCO; recomputing
against the corrected baseline retracted an earlier, broader claim of a
universal, compliance-independent utility collapse. Second, this
dissertation's text-normalization function was found deleting
punctuation rather than replacing it with a space, silently
undercounting matches on answers like "28-32" against "28 - 32"; this
affected 8.9\% of combined Phase 1 and injection rows, concentrated on
MS MARCO, and this dissertation corrected it directly from stored raw
output.

Ministral-3-8B's result required a dedicated diagnostic before it could
be reported with confidence, since a real possibility existed that its
figures reflected a mechanical tokenizer artifact rather than genuine
compliance. Direct inspection of all 607 of its flagged rows under
\texttt{ignore} and \texttt{fake\_completion} found zero instances of
the model abandoning the actual question, every one still attempted a
correct answer alongside printing the injected marker text. A pure
tokenizer round-trip diagnostic, rendering the real attack prompt,
tokenizing it, decoding it back, and comparing byte-for-byte against the
original, using Ministral-3-8B's actual pinned tokenizer, confirmed both
templates round-trip identically: \texttt{ignore} produced a 283-token,
1,283-character prompt with an exact decoded match, and
\texttt{fake\_completion} produced 280 tokens and 1,272 characters with
the same exact match.

This result directly disconfirms the specific hypothesized mechanism, a
tokenizer round-trip corrupting or duplicating content near the injected
text: what the model receives is exactly what was intended. The
30.5--60.8\% figures therefore describe genuine, if partial, compliance
with the injected print-instruction, not a rendering artifact and not
full task abandonment, which never occurs in this dataset.

This distinction matters because rule-based substring-match ASR scoring
conflates three distinct phenomena under one binary flag,
confirmed by manual inspection of flagged rows across all four models:
verbatim echo of the injected text, the injected marker glued onto an
otherwise unaffected answer attempt (the pattern behind Ministral-3-8B's
figures), and explicit reasoning leading to a deliberate compliant
choice, the rarest pattern, found only twice, both for Phi-4-mini. This
is a real limitation of this scoring method for this attack class, not
a footnote specific to one model: the metric correctly flags all three
patterns as "target string present" without distinguishing which one
actually occurred, and only the percentage figures combined with this
row-level inspection give an accurate picture of what a given ASR number
represents.

ASR runs 70--78\% on HotpotQA versus 18--20\% on MS MARCO, consistently
across all four models. All four corpus-versus-corpus comparisons reach
significance at $p<2\times10^{-12}$ (Fisher's exact test), while the
smallest of twelve model-versus-model comparisons has a raw $p=0.065$,
which does not survive Holm-Bonferroni correction. Retrieval precision,
the fraction of the top-5 retrieved documents that are the planted
poison, reaches 99.3\% on HotpotQA versus 88.96\% on MS MARCO. Mean
question length is 18.17 words on HotpotQA versus 6.14 words on MS
MARCO. A verified skip rate of 14 of 1,472 target questions was
confirmed directly against the live sweep log: nine truncation failures,
three infrastructure failures, one content-policy refusal, and one
since-fixed parser gap.

Here corpus identity dominates rather than model identity, the mirror
image of the injection attack's pattern; that injection is dominated by
model identity while PoisonedRAG is dominated by corpus identity
suggests these are genuinely independent capabilities, not two views of
one underlying "robustness" property. Two mechanisms, both measured
directly rather than inferred, plausibly explain why the corpus effect
is this large, and this dissertation does not claim to know their
relative weight. On MS MARCO, real content still competes for roughly
one in nine retrieved slots, which may explain the lower ASR there.
HotpotQA's longer, multi-clause, multi-entity questions may also give a
crafted, roughly 100-word poison passage far more surface to weave a
persuasive fabricated answer into than a short, generic query permits.
Selecting a poison-generator model itself surfaced a real methodological
finding, unrelated to the attack's substantive results: two earlier
generator and transport combinations were tried and abandoned for
concrete, verified engineering reasons, one measured 480 seconds for a
trivial reply, not viable at sweep scale, and another's advertised free
API tier turned out to enforce a ten-cent-per-month cap in practice,
before settling on Nemotron-3-Ultra via the NVIDIA NIM API as the
poison-passage generator used throughout.

\section{Attack 3: Crescendo}

This attack runs a single escalation strategy across five turns for each
of four target models, against a stratified 100-behavior sample per
model drawn from a pooled 500-behavior set. DeepSeek V4 Pro plays both
attacker and judge in every conversation and is never itself a target.
Figure~\ref{fig:crescendo-asr} displays the resulting ASR with bootstrap
confidence intervals; Table~\ref{tab:crescendo-full} breaks down each
model individually.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{$n$} & \textbf{$n$ Scored} & \textbf{ASR} & \textbf{Backtrack Rate} & \textbf{Mean Refusals} \\
\midrule
Llama-3.1-8B & 100 & 94 & 70.21\% & 44.0\% & 0.91 \\
Ministral-3-8B & 100 & 95 & 69.47\% & 2.0\% & 0.02 \\
Qwen3-8B & 100 & 95 & 68.42\% & 23.0\% & 0.28 \\
Phi-4-mini & 100 & 98 & 59.18\% & 23.0\% & 0.29 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Crescendo full per-model results: attack success rate against refusal and backtrack behavior.}
\label{tab:crescendo-full}
\end{table}

None of six pairwise ASR comparisons survive Holm-Bonferroni correction,
the closest being Llama-3.1-8B versus Phi-4-mini at a raw $p=0.0428$
against a corrected threshold of $p\leq0.0083$. A bootstrap 95\%
confidence interval on that same gap, 0.129 [0.011, 0.237], excludes
zero even though the corrected significance test does not. DeepSeek's
high compliance as attacker, verified in a pre-flight check across ten
real five-turn escalation calls with no refusal, is expected generator
behavior; every result above describes only the four target models.

The flat aggregate ASR ranking masks a strong, distinct per-model
mechanism signature. Llama-3.1-8B refuses constantly, the highest
refusal rate of the four, and backtracks in 44\% of behaviors, yet still
lands at the single highest ASR, because backtracking reliably breaks
through its own resistance. Ministral-3-8B sits at the opposite extreme,
almost never refusing at all, and lands at essentially the same ASR
regardless, since there is barely any resistance for the attack to
overcome in the first place. Qwen3-8B and Phi-4-mini both refuse at an
intermediate rate but diverge in outcome: Qwen3-8B's backtracks convert
to success about as readily as Llama-3.1-8B's, while Phi-4-mini's
comparable resistance holds its position more often, consistent with
its lowest ASR among the four. This outcome resembles PoisonedRAG's
pattern in one specific sense, no significant model ranking despite a
real, substantial ASR, but the mechanism underneath resembles the
injection attack's pattern instead: two models can land at nearly the
same aggregate ASR through entirely different behavioral paths, which
means a single intervention tuned to fix one model's failure mode may
do nothing for the other.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig05_crescendo_asr_bootstrap_ci}
\caption{Crescendo ASR by model, with bootstrap confidence intervals.}
\label{fig:crescendo-asr}
\end{figure}

The refusal-detection function underlying the backtrack counts above is
a substring scan with a confirmed false-positive mode: both of
Ministral-3-8B's two backtrack rows in the entire sweep were
individually audited and found to be false positives, the target's
reply was already substantively compliant in both cases, and the
matched text sat inside fabricated dialogue the model itself generated,
not an actual refusal. This audit was tractable only for Ministral-3-8B,
whose two flagged rows made an exhaustive check practical; the other
three models' 23-to-44 flagged rows each were not individually audited.

Refusal and backtrack counts reported throughout this section should
therefore be read as an upper bound on genuine resistance, not an exact
figure. Since Ministral-3-8B's own two flagged rows turned out to be
entirely false positives, its already-near-zero refusal rate may in
fact overstate its true resistance rather than understate it, though
this dissertation cannot confirm that without auditing the other three
models' larger flagged-row sets.

Model identity, corpus identity, and neither turn out to be three
distinct statistical axes, not three flavors of one property called
"robustness." This has a load-bearing implication for how a defense
should be judged: a defense evaluated only on aggregate Crescendo ASR
reduction would implicitly treat all four models as an equally good
starting point, when Llama-3.1-8B and Ministral-3-8B in fact need
opposite interventions, one needs its refusals reinforced to survive
rephrasing, the other needs to be brought to refuse at all. This same
logic extends across all three attacks: a robustness claim phrased as
"Model X is robust" or "Model X is vulnerable," without naming the
attack family and, where relevant, the corpus, is not a claim this
dissertation's evidence actually supports. Robustness in this dataset is
always a property of a specific model-attack, or model-attack-corpus,
combination, never a single number attached to a model in isolation.

Of 1,511 items \texttt{output\_filter} appeared to block before
correction, only 12 (0.8\%) show an actual guard flag behind them. The
cells with the largest, most significant uncorrected reductions, all
Ministral-3-8B, 303 to 525 blocked items each, have the lowest
guard-catch fractions among all cells, 0.2 to 1.1\%. The guard's flag
rate against PoisonedRAG is exactly 0.0000 across all 160 scored
responses, every model, every corpus. \texttt{spotlighting} keeps 9 of
16 injection cells and 4 of 24 PoisonedRAG cells significant after
correction. Only Qwen3-8B's three significant cells show a backend
contribution close to zero; the remaining six cells still owe 50--86\%
of their originally reported reduction to the backend switch itself.
Spotlighting costs a mean $\Delta F_1$ of $-0.283$ against injection, as
low as $-0.44$ in some cells. \texttt{instruction\_detection} reaches 0
of 16 significant injection cells at $n=40$, down from 3 of 16 under the
original comparator, all three confirmed as the same backend-confound
pattern. Of the 55 items this defense originally appeared to block, only
10 survive correction as genuine blocks, and all 10 (100\%) have at
least one retrieved passage the classifier actually flagged. Against
Crescendo, \texttt{output\_filter}'s guard verdict is logged but never
acted on, so no defended-condition ASR reduction exists to report for
that pairing; instead, of conversations that succeeded unfiltered, 67 to
82\% had at least one turn the guard would have flagged, a diagnostic
figure discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:article15-mapping} below.

The backend confound explains the \texttt{output\_filter} pattern
directly: nearly all of the apparent defense effect was the backend
switch itself, not guard action, the exact opposite of what "the guard
is doing the defending" would predict given which cells showed the
largest uncorrected reductions. The correction reverses the headline
conclusion entirely: what appeared to be a working defense before
correction is, once the confound is removed, no defense at all. Against
PoisonedRAG specifically, the mismatch is structural rather than a
matter of degree: a safety-content classifier structurally cannot see
factual poisoning, since a poisoned answer is not unsafe, it is simply
wrong, a distinction orthogonal to what a guard-style classifier
screens for. No amount of retuning this classifier's threshold would
fix this, since the problem is not miscalibration, it is that
PoisonedRAG's output does not belong to the category of content the
classifier was ever built to recognize. \texttt{output\_filter}'s
67--82\% counterfactual catch rate against Crescendo is an encouraging
signal for a future, actually wired version of this defense, since
Crescendo's attack surface is exactly what a safety classifier is built
to catch, unlike PoisonedRAG's factual corruption.

\texttt{spotlighting} is therefore this dissertation's clearest example
of a defense that genuinely works and genuinely costs something, rather
than either working for free or failing outright, and its confirmed
effect is more modest than the original uncorrected headline suggested.
The headline conclusion survives correction here, just at a smaller
magnitude: spotlighting was, and remains, the one defense in this
dissertation with a confirmed real effect.

\texttt{instruction\_detection}'s mechanism evidence tells a different
story than its significance test alone: this is underpowered, not
disproven, and the $n=40$ cap reflects a real compute constraint, a
transformer classifier run once per retrieved passage on CPU, rather
than a design choice, a real-hardware timing measurement found a single
item took four minutes and twenty-seven seconds wall-clock even after
fixing a CPU thread-oversubscription bug. The correction does not settle
this defense's headline conclusion either way: the significance test
says no effect, the mechanism evidence says otherwise, and this
dissertation reports both rather than favoring one.

Figure~\ref{fig:fp-check} captures the false-positive side of
\texttt{instruction\_detection}: it flags 4.7\% of benign HotpotQA

\citep{rottger2024xstest}, and 2.7\% of OR-Bench's hard-but-safe prompts
\citep{cui2025orbench}.

\section{Efficiency: A Named Scope Boundary}

This dissertation measures robustness only. It does not measure
inference latency, throughput, memory footprint, or any other
speed-versus-robustness trade-off for any of the four models or three
defenses, and this is a genuine limitation of the evaluation as it
stands, not an oversight to gloss over. A practitioner weighing this
dissertation's robustness findings against a real deployment decision
would still need a separate efficiency evaluation this dissertation does
not provide. Spotlighting's base64 encoding, for instance, adds
per-request overhead that this dissertation never measured, and this
dissertation never characterized instruction-detection's per-passage
classifier cost at production scale either; Chapter 4 reports a single
4-minute-27-second wall-clock timing, collected to explain that
defense's sample-size cap, not to serve as a throughput benchmark, and
it should not be read as one. This dissertation names
robustness-versus-efficiency trade-off measurement explicitly as a
concrete, well-motivated direction for future work, addressed further in
Chapter 6, instead of folding it silently into its own robustness
conclusions.

\section{Comparison to Related Benchmarks}

Chapter 3 introduced three comparator benchmarks against which this
dissertation's design was positioned; this section returns to two of
them where a direct numerical comparison of findings, not just design
scope, is possible. RSB's own headline defense finding, that its
strongest defense reduces poisoning success but at an accuracy cost
exceeding 20\% relative to its clean baseline in most settings, parallels
this dissertation's spotlighting result almost exactly in shape: a
confirmed real effect purchased at a real, substantial utility cost,
even though the two benchmarks measure entirely different attack
families and use entirely different metrics. That two independent
benchmarks, built by different teams on different attacks, converge on
the same pattern, a real defense effect and full utility preservation
essentially never arriving together, is a stronger basis for that claim
than either result alone would provide. Neither SafeRAG nor
Hidden-in-Plain-Text report a comparably structured defense-utility
number, so no equivalent direct comparison is possible against those two;
this is itself consistent with Chapter 3's positioning of this
dissertation's design as the only one of the four to test defenses under
a shared, utility-tracked, backend-controlled setup across three attack
families at once.

\section{Implications for Practitioners}

Four implications follow directly from the results above, each grounded
in a specific finding rather than stated as general advice. First, a
practitioner should not assume a defense's effectiveness generalizes
across attack types: \texttt{output\_filter} appeared to strongly reduce
injection ASR before backend correction, and that apparent effect
collapsed to 0 of 19 cells once the confound was isolated, a direct
cautionary example against trusting an uncorrected before/after
comparison at face value. Second, a practitioner should match the
defense to the actual threat model instead of assuming a well-built
defense transfers automatically: \texttt{output\_filter}'s 0.0000 flag
rate against PoisonedRAG is not a tuning problem, a safety-content
classifier has no mechanism to see factual poisoning by construction,
and this dissertation's data shows a defense can be competently built
and still be the wrong tool for a given attack surface. Third, a
practitioner should treat a small-sample result with caution: the
\texttt{instruction\_detection} $n=40$ cap reflects a real compute
constraint this dissertation encountered, not a design choice, and its
0 of 16 significant result at that sample size cannot, on this
dissertation's own evidence, be distinguished from a real but
underpowered effect, given that 10 of 10 backend-corrected blocks carry
a genuine classifier fingerprint. Fourth, the EU AI Act Article 15
compliance-testing gap discussed above is current and unresolved, not a
historical footnote, and this dissertation's own mapping speaks to it
directly instead of gesturing at it in the abstract.

\section{Answering the Research Questions}

RQ1 asked how vulnerable these four models are to each attack family.
The answer is not a single number but a structure: vulnerability is
real and substantial for every attack tested, but which model is most
at risk depends entirely on which attack and, for two of the three
attacks, which corpus, is in question. Ministral-3-8B is the most
exposed model under injection, all four models are near-equally exposed
under PoisonedRAG once corpus is held constant, and no model is
reliably safer than any other under Crescendo, though the four differ
sharply in the behavioral mechanism behind that shared ASR range. A
single aggregate robustness score across all three attacks would average
away exactly the structure this dissertation's results say matters
most.

RQ2 asked how effective practical, low-cost defenses are, and at what
cost. Across three defenses tested against two of the three attack
families, exactly one, spotlighting against injection, clears the bar of
a confirmed, backend-isolated real effect, and it does so at a
non-trivial utility cost. \texttt{output\_filter} clears no such bar
against either attack it was tested on. \texttt{instruction\_detection}'s
result is genuinely mixed: statistically unconfirmed at its available
sample size, but mechanistically real. No defense tested here achieves
both a confirmed effect and full utility preservation at once, which is
this dissertation's central answer to RQ2: low-cost, practical defenses
in this space are not yet free, and the honest way to report a
defense's value is as a pair of numbers, effect and cost, never one
number alone.
