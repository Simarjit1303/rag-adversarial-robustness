\chapter{Evaluation and Results}

\section{Experimental Setup}

This chapter reports results from the benchmark design established in
Chapters 3 and 4: four target models, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
\citep{grattafiori2024llama3}, Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3},
Phi-4-mini-instruct \citep{abouelenin2025phi4mini}, and
Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512 \citep{mistralai2025ministral3}, evaluated
across two retrieval corpora, HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa} and MS
MARCO \citep{nguyen2016msmarco}, against three attack families, indirect
prompt injection \citep{greshake2023not}, PoisonedRAG
\citep{zou2025poisonedrag}, and Crescendo \citep{russinovich2025great},
and three defenses, spotlighting \citep{hines2024defending},
instruction-detection, and output-filter using Llama Guard 4
\citep{metaai2025llamaguard4}. Every generation reported in this chapter
ran on real GPU hardware rather than simulated or estimated output, and
every reported statistic uses paired testing appropriate to its
comparison type: McNemar's exact test for paired same-corpus model
comparisons, Fisher's exact test for unpaired corpus-versus-corpus
comparisons, and Holm-Bonferroni correction applied within each family
of hypotheses to control for the number of comparisons made. Attack
success is measured as attack success rate (ASR), the proportion of
attempts in which the attack achieves its stated objective; defense
effectiveness is measured as the change in ASR under a defended
condition relative to the matched undefended baseline; and utility cost
is measured as the change in answer-quality F1 under a defended
condition relative to the clean, no-attack baseline reported in
Section~\ref{sec:baseline-quality}.

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\texttt{nq\_open}}{nq\_open} Exclusion}

This dissertation originally intended Natural Questions Open
\citep{kwiatkowski2019natural} as a third retrieval corpus alongside
HotpotQA and MS MARCO. Figure~\ref{fig:nq-open-exclusion} shows why it
was dropped from every real sweep instead. The technical finding is a
corpus-construction fact, not a model or implementation failure: no
genuine supporting passage ships with \texttt{nq\_open}, so its passage
text \emph{is} the gold answer by construction. The evidence is
unambiguous: mean F1 of 0.962 (spread 0.005) on \texttt{nq\_open} versus
0.585 (spread 0.179) on HotpotQA and 0.255 (spread 0.042) on MS MARCO. A
score this close to ceiling, and this invariant, across four models of
markedly different capability, is not a sign of genuine task difficulty
being solved uniformly well, it is the signature of a corpus that leaks
its own answer.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig02_nq_open_exclusion_evidence}
\caption{Evidence for the \texttt{nq\_open} exclusion: near-ceiling, near-invariant F1 across all four models.}
\label{fig:nq-open-exclusion}
\end{figure}

The scope impact of this exclusion is narrower than "losing a corpus"
outright: Crescendo is corpus-independent and entirely unaffected. Only
the two corpus-dependent attack families, indirect prompt injection and
PoisonedRAG, lose one of three corpora, reducing their design to a
4-model-by-2-corpus matrix rather than 4-by-3.\footnote{The dissertation
supervisor confirmed and approved this exclusion as a settled scope
decision. The technical reasoning above, not the approval itself, is the
argument for it.} The real matrix going forward, then, is four models by
two corpora by three attacks, with Crescendo unaffected by the corpus
reduction.

\section{Attack 1: Indirect Prompt Injection}

This attack tests four models across two corpora against five injection
templates, organized into two categories: goal-hijacking, which redirects
the model to a different question entirely (\texttt{naive},
\texttt{escape\_char}, \texttt{combined}), and process-hijacking, which
instead convinces the model it is at a different stage of its own task
(\texttt{ignore}, \texttt{fake\_completion}). This taxonomy is a design
decision made for this benchmark rather than a direct import from
\citet{liu2024formalizing}'s original formulation. This attack places
the injection text into an organically retrieved document rather than
forcing it into the top-$k$ results, keeping this attack's surface
distinct from PoisonedRAG's forced-retrieval mechanism.
Figure~\ref{fig:injection-asr} visualizes the resulting pattern;
Table~\ref{tab:injection-full} lists every cell.

Two corrections apply to every number above, both already incorporated.
First, the Phase 1 baseline used for utility-drop comparisons was
originally inflated by a dictionary-representation leak, the same
category of corpus-construction issue behind the \texttt{nq\_open}
exclusion, before this benchmark fixed it for both HotpotQA and MS
MARCO; recomputing against the corrected baseline retracted an earlier,
broader claim of a universal, compliance-independent utility collapse.
Second, this dissertation found its own text-normalization function
deleting punctuation rather than replacing it with a space, silently
undercounting matches on answers like "28-32" against "28 - 32"; this
affected 8.9\% of combined Phase 1 and injection rows, concentrated on
MS MARCO, and this dissertation corrected it directly from stored raw
output. Neither correction changes any ASR number in
Table~\ref{tab:injection-full} or any significance verdict, since ASR
here is a raw substring check untouched by either fix.

The headline finding once these corrections are applied is that utility
damage tracks compliance rather than existing as a separate
context-pollution effect. Across the three goal-hijack templates, where
ASR sits at 0.1-1.1\% almost everywhere, the F1 change against the
corrected baseline is small and inconsistent in sign, every one of 24
goal-hijack cells falls between $-0.038$ and $+0.025$. Utility damage
concentrates instead in exactly the cells that also show elevated ASR,
the two process-hijack templates, and within those, specifically the
models showing real compliance. One exception survives as a genuinely
compliance-independent cost: Phi-4-mini on HotpotQA under
\texttt{fake\_completion} shows a real F1 drop of 0.024 (95\% CI
0.010-0.037) alongside near-zero ASR (0.2\%), an order of magnitude
smaller than an earlier, broader estimate of this effect.

Figure~\ref{fig:injection-asr} presents indirect prompt injection ASR by
model and injection template. The four models respond to this attack in
four genuinely different ways, not a uniform degree of vulnerability:
Llama-3.1-8B remains near-zero everywhere, the most injection-robust of
the four models tested here; Qwen3-8B is vulnerable specifically to the
\texttt{fake\_completion} template but largely resists \texttt{ignore},
hypothesized, though not proven, to relate to Qwen3's explicit
thinking-mode chat template being more susceptible to a forged
completion signal than a blunt instruction override; Phi-4-mini is
vulnerable to \texttt{fake\_completion} on MS MARCO only, a corpus split
confirmed statistically real ($p=8.18\times10^{-29}$), hypothesized to
relate to MS MARCO's shorter, less clearly bounded passage fragments
giving a forged signal more room to blend in; and Ministral-3-8B is the
most susceptible model overall, vulnerable to both process-hijack
templates.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig03_injection_asr_model_template}
\caption{Indirect injection ASR by model and template.}
\label{fig:injection-asr}
\end{figure}

Ministral-3-8B's result required a dedicated diagnostic before it could
be reported with confidence. Direct inspection of all 607 of its flagged
rows under \texttt{ignore} and \texttt{fake\_completion} found zero
instances of the model abandoning the actual question, every one still
attempted a correct answer alongside printing the injected marker text,
which raised a real possibility that the figures reflected a mechanical
artifact of this model's specific tokenizer and chat-template class
rather than genuine compliance. This diagnostic needed no GPU access,
it is a pure tokenizer operation: rendering the real attack prompt,
tokenizing it, and decoding it back, then comparing the result against
the original prompt byte-for-byte, using Ministral-3-8B's actual pinned
tokenizer. Both templates round-tripped identically: \texttt{ignore}
produced a 283-token, 1,283-character rendered prompt with the decoded
string matching the original exactly, and \texttt{fake\_completion}
produced 280 tokens and 1,272 characters with the same exact match. This
result directly disconfirms the specific hypothesized mechanism, a
tokenizer round-trip corrupting or duplicating content near the injected
text: what the model receives is exactly what was intended. The
30.5-60.8\% figures therefore describe genuine, if partial, compliance
with the injected print-instruction, not a rendering artifact and not
full task abandonment, which never occurs in this dataset.

This distinction matters because rule-based substring-match ASR scoring
conflates three genuinely different phenomena under one binary flag,
confirmed by manual inspection of flagged rows across all four models:
verbatim echo of the injected text, the injected marker glued onto an
otherwise unaffected answer attempt (the pattern behind Ministral-3-8B's
figures), and explicit reasoning leading to a deliberate compliant
choice, the rarest pattern, found only twice, both for Phi-4-mini. This
is a real limitation of this scoring method for this attack class, not
a footnote specific to one model: the metric correctly flags all three
patterns as "target string present" without distinguishing which one
actually occurred, and only the percentage figures combined with this
row-level inspection give an accurate picture of what a given ASR number
represents.

Here corpus identity dominates rather than model identity, the mirror
image of the injection attack's pattern: ASR runs 70-78\% on HotpotQA
versus 18-20\% on MS MARCO, consistently across all four models. All
four corpus-versus-corpus comparisons reach significance at
$p<2\times10^{-12}$ (Fisher's exact test), while the smallest of twelve
model-versus-model comparisons has a raw $p=0.065$, which does not
survive Holm-Bonferroni correction. That injection is dominated by model
identity while PoisonedRAG is dominated by corpus identity suggests
these are genuinely independent capabilities, not two views of one
underlying "robustness" property.

Two mechanisms, measured directly rather than inferred, plausibly
explain why the corpus effect is this large, and this dissertation does
not claim to know their relative weight. First, retrieval precision, the
fraction of the top-5 retrieved documents that are the planted poison,
reaches 99.3\% on HotpotQA versus 88.96\% on MS MARCO; on MS MARCO, real
content still competes for roughly one in nine retrieved slots. Second,
mean question length is 18.17 words on HotpotQA versus 6.14 words on MS
MARCO; HotpotQA's longer, multi-clause, multi-entity questions give a
crafted, roughly 100-word poison passage far more surface to weave a
persuasive fabricated answer into than a short, generic query permits.

This dissertation verified a small skip rate, 14 of 1,472 target
questions, directly against the live sweep log rather than
reconstructing it after the fact. Nine of the fourteen were truncation
failures, the poison-generator model's internal reasoning competing with
its output for token budget; three were API or infrastructure failures
unrelated to content; one was a genuine content-policy refusal; and one
traced to a since-fixed parser formatting gap. Selecting a
poison-generator model itself surfaced a real methodological finding:
this dissertation tried and abandoned two earlier generator and
transport combinations for concrete, verified engineering reasons, one
measured 480 seconds for a trivial reply, not viable at sweep scale, and
another's advertised free API tier turned out to enforce a
ten-cent-per-month cap in practice, before settling on Nemotron-3-Ultra
via the NVIDIA NIM API as the poison-passage generator used throughout.

\section{Attack 3: Crescendo}

This attack runs a single escalation strategy across five turns for each
of four target models, against a stratified 100-behavior sample per
model drawn from a pooled 500-behavior set. DeepSeek V4 Pro plays both
attacker and judge in every conversation and is never itself a target;
its high compliance as attacker, verified in a pre-flight check across
ten real five-turn escalation calls with no refusal, is expected
generator behavior, not a susceptibility finding about DeepSeek itself.
Every result below describes only the four target models.

Figure~\ref{fig:crescendo-asr} displays the resulting ASR with bootstrap
confidence intervals; Table~\ref{tab:crescendo-full} breaks down each
model individually, including the refusal and backtrack rates behind
each aggregate number.

The outcome resembles PoisonedRAG's pattern, no significant model
ranking, since none of six pairwise comparisons survive Holm-Bonferroni
correction, the closest being Llama-3.1-8B versus Phi-4-mini at a raw
$p=0.0428$ against a corrected threshold of $p\leq0.0083$. But the
mechanism underneath that flat outcome resembles the injection attack's
pattern instead, a strong, distinct per-model signature: Llama-3.1-8B
refuses constantly, the highest refusal rate of the four, and backtracks
in 44\% of behaviors, yet still lands at the single highest ASR, because
backtracking reliably breaks through its own resistance. Ministral-3-8B
sits at the opposite extreme, almost never refusing at all, and lands at
essentially the same ASR regardless, since there is barely any
resistance for the attack to overcome in the first place. Qwen3-8B and
Phi-4-mini both refuse at an intermediate rate but diverge in outcome:
Qwen3-8B's backtracks convert to success about as readily as
Llama-3.1-8B's, while Phi-4-mini's comparable resistance holds its
position more often, consistent with its lowest ASR among the four; a
bootstrap 95\% confidence interval on the gap between Phi-4-mini and
Llama-3.1-8B, 0.129 [0.011, 0.237], excludes zero even though the
Holm-corrected significance test on the same pair does not.

The refusal-detection function underlying these backtrack counts is a
substring scan with a confirmed false-positive mode. This dissertation
individually audited both of Ministral-3-8B's two backtrack rows in the
entire sweep and found them to be false positives, the target's reply
was already substantively compliant in both cases, and the matched text
sat inside fabricated dialogue the model itself generated, not an actual
refusal. Refusal and backtrack counts should therefore be read as an
upper bound on genuine resistance; this audit was tractable only for
Ministral-3-8B, whose two flagged rows made an exhaustive check
practical, and the other three models' 23-to-44 flagged rows each were
not individually audited.

Table~\ref{tab:cross-attack-axes} brings the three attacks together.
Each is dominated by a different axis: model identity for injection,
corpus identity for PoisonedRAG, and neither for Crescendo, where no
model pairing survives correction at all.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Attack} & \textbf{Dominant axis} & \textbf{Statistical support} \\
\midrule
Indirect injection & Model identity & Model-specific template vulnerability \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Three attacks, three different dominant axes.}
\label{tab:cross-attack-axes}

This has a load-bearing implication for how a defense should be judged.
A defense evaluated only on aggregate Crescendo ASR reduction would
implicitly treat all four models as an equally good starting point, when
Llama-3.1-8B and Ministral-3-8B in fact need opposite interventions: one
needs its refusals reinforced to survive rephrasing, the other needs to
be brought to refuse at all. This same logic extends across all three
attacks. A robustness claim phrased as "Model X is robust" or "Model X
is vulnerable," without naming the attack family and, where relevant,
the corpus, is not a claim this dissertation's evidence actually
supports; robustness in this dataset is always a property of a specific
model-attack, or model-attack-corpus, combination, never a single
number attached to a model in isolation.

\section{Defense Evaluation and the Backend-Confound Discovery}

Figures~\ref{fig:backend-correction} and~\ref{fig:backend-verdicts}
visualize the backend-confound correction introduced methodologically in
Chapter 4; this section reports its consequences for each defense
individually, since the effect differs sharply by defense rather than
applying uniformly. This correction is, in effect, this dissertation's
sensitivity analysis: rather than varying a tunable hyperparameter, it
tests how sensitive each defense's reported effect is to a single
infrastructure variable, the inference backend, and the answer differs
completely by defense, which is itself evidence that the original,
uncorrected numbers could not be trusted as reported.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig06_phase3_backend_correction_before_after}
\caption{Backend-confound correction: significant cells before and after, by defense.}
\label{fig:backend-correction}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig07_phase3_backend_confound_verdicts}
\caption{Backend-confound verdicts across defense-attack combinations.}
\label{fig:backend-verdicts}
\end{figure}

\texttt{output\_filter} shows no significant effect on injection, 0 of
19 cells, down from 6 of 19 under the original, confound-contaminated
comparison, or PoisonedRAG, 0 of 24 cells, after correction. Direct
mechanism evidence explains why: of 1,511 items \texttt{output\_filter}
appeared to block before correction, only 12 (0.8\%) show an actual
guard flag behind them. The cells with the largest, most significant
uncorrected reductions, all Ministral-3-8B, 303 to 525 blocked items
each, have the lowest guard-catch fractions among all cells, 0.2 to
1.1\%, the exact opposite of what "the guard is doing the defending"
would predict. The backend confound explains this directly: nearly all
of the apparent defense effect was the backend switch itself, not guard
action.

Against PoisonedRAG specifically, the mismatch is structural rather than
a matter of degree: the guard's flag rate is exactly 0.0000 across all
160 scored PoisonedRAG responses, every model, every corpus. A
safety-content classifier structurally cannot see factual poisoning, a
poisoned answer is not unsafe, it is simply wrong, and that distinction
is orthogonal to what a guard-style classifier screens for. No amount of
retuning this classifier's threshold would fix this, since the problem
is not miscalibration, it is that PoisonedRAG's output does not belong
to the category of content the classifier was ever built to recognize.

Against Crescendo, \texttt{output\_filter} is not a defended-condition
measurement at all, and is correctly excluded from every ASR-reduction
table for that reason: by design, the guard's verdict is logged but
never acted on, the judge always scores the real, unfiltered
conversation, so there is no live intervention to measure a reduction
from. What was measured instead is purely diagnostic: of conversations
that succeeded unfiltered, 67 to 82\% had at least one turn the guard
would have flagged before the conversation concluded, broken down by
model as 72.7\% for Llama-3.1-8B, 81.8\% for Qwen3-8B, 66.7\% for
Phi-4-mini, and 73.3\% for Ministral-3-8B. This is a genuinely
encouraging number for a future, actually wired version of this
defense, since Crescendo's attack surface, multi-turn escalation toward
unsafe content, is exactly what a safety classifier is built to catch,
unlike PoisonedRAG's factual corruption. This remains a well-motivated,
unexplored next step, discussed further in Chapter 6, not a hidden gap
in what was tested here.

PoisonedRAG cells remain significant after correction.

pair, still owe 50-86\% of their originally reported reduction to the
backend switch itself. Second, spotlighting costs a mean $\Delta F_1$ of
$-0.283$ against injection, as low as $-0.44$ in some cells, a real
utility cost alongside the real effect. Taken together, spotlighting is
this dissertation's clearest example of a defense that genuinely works
and genuinely costs something, rather than either working for free or
failing outright.

\texttt{instruction\_detection} reaches 0 of 16 significant injection
cells at its capped sample size of $n=40$, down from 3 of 16 under the
original comparator, all three of which were Ministral-3-8B cells and
all three confirmed as the same backend-confound pattern seen in
\texttt{output\_filter}, not \texttt{spotlighting}. Mechanism attribution
tells a different story than the significance test alone: of the 55
items this defense originally appeared to block, only 10 survive the
backend correction as genuine blocks, and all 10 (100\%) have at least
one retrieved passage the classifier actually flagged. The other 45 were
never really blocked by the classifier at all, the model's answer had
simply changed between the mismatched and matched backends, independent
of any defense action. The confound inflated the apparent block count
roughly 4.5-fold; it did not obscure a real classifier failure, every
genuine block in this dataset carries the classifier's fingerprint. This
is underpowered, not disproven. The $n=40$ cap, against 1,000 for the
other two defenses, reflects a real compute constraint, a transformer
classifier run once per retrieved passage on CPU, rather than a design
choice: a real-hardware timing measurement found a single item took
four minutes and twenty-seven seconds wall-clock even after fixing a
CPU thread-oversubscription bug, making the full $n=1{,}000$ sweep
impractical within this project's compute budget.

Figure~\ref{fig:fp-check} captures the false-positive side of this
defense, functioning as this dissertation's robustness check against
inputs the classifier was never tuned on: \texttt{instruction\_detection}
flags 4.7\% of benign HotpotQA inputs, 3.3\% of benign MS MARCO inputs,
0.0\% of XSTest's safe prompts \citep{rottger2024xstest}, and 2.7\% of
OR-Bench's hard-but-safe prompts \citep{cui2025orbench}. The 0.0\% result
on XSTest is worth noting specifically as a favorable result, but the
4.7\% rate on real corpus passages shows that a classifier's performance
on a curated benchmark of adversarially designed safe prompts does not
automatically predict its behavior on ordinary retrieved text, which is
exactly the gap pairing two different false-positive sources was meant
to expose.

\section{Scope Not Evaluated: Efficiency}

This dissertation measures robustness only. It does not measure
inference latency, throughput, memory footprint, or any other
speed-versus-robustness trade-off for any of the four models or three
defenses, and this is a genuine limitation of the evaluation as it
stands, not an oversight to gloss over. A practitioner weighing this
dissertation's robustness findings against a real deployment decision
would still need a separate efficiency evaluation this dissertation does
not provide. Spotlighting's base64 encoding, for instance, adds
per-request overhead that this dissertation never measured, and this
dissertation never characterized instruction-detection's per-passage
classifier cost at production scale either; Chapter 4 reports a single
4-minute-27-second wall-clock timing, collected to explain that
defense's sample-size cap, not to serve as a throughput benchmark, and
it should not be read as one. This dissertation names
robustness-versus-efficiency trade-off measurement explicitly as a
concrete, well-motivated direction for future work, addressed further in
Chapter 6, instead of folding it silently into its own robustness
conclusions.

\section{Comparison to Related Benchmarks}

Chapter 3 introduced three comparator benchmarks against which this
dissertation's design was positioned; this section returns to two of
them where a direct numerical comparison of findings, not just design
scope, is possible. RSB's own headline defense finding, that its
strongest defense reduces poisoning success but at an accuracy cost
exceeding 20\% relative to its clean baseline in most settings, parallels
this dissertation's spotlighting result almost exactly in shape: a
confirmed real effect purchased at a real, substantial utility cost,
even though the two benchmarks measure entirely different attack
families and use entirely different metrics. That two independent
benchmarks, built by different teams on different attacks, converge on
the same pattern, a real defense effect and full utility preservation
essentially never arriving together, is a stronger basis for that claim
than either result alone would provide. Neither SafeRAG nor
Hidden-in-Plain-Text report a comparably structured defense-utility
number, so no equivalent direct comparison is possible against those two;
this is itself consistent with Chapter 3's positioning of this
dissertation's design as the only one of the four to test defenses under
a shared, utility-tracked, backend-controlled setup across three attack
families at once.

The Crescendo mapping carries one further, named engineering gap:
\texttt{output\_filter} against Crescendo is diagnostic-only by design,
its guard verdict is logged but never fed back to block a response. In
67-82\% of successful Crescendo attacks, some turn in the conversation
had already triggered a guard flag that, had the system been wired to
enforce it, would have stopped the attack. This is reported as a named
engineering gap, not a completed defense test.

The "no harmonised standard" claim running through this mapping is not
a vague assertion; it has a specific, checkable status. As of the
verification underlying this dissertation's own regulatory mapping, the
CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 harmonised standards intended to cover Article 15's
accuracy and robustness requirement, \texttt{prEN 18229-2}, and its
cybersecurity requirement, \texttt{prEN 18282}, both remain in draft
status and have not been published in the Official Journal. No
presumption-of-conformity testing standard therefore exists yet for the
exact requirement this dissertation's own methodology maps against.
This dissertation does not claim to fill that standards gap; it
documents, with a concrete worked example, what the gap looks like from
the evaluation side, a narrower and more defensible claim than asserting
this benchmark itself constitutes or anticipates the eventual standard.

\section{Implications for Practitioners}

Four implications follow directly from the results above, each grounded
in a specific finding rather than stated as general advice. First, a
practitioner should not assume a defense's effectiveness generalizes
across attack types: \texttt{output\_filter} appeared to strongly reduce
injection ASR before backend correction, and that apparent effect
collapsed to 0 of 19 cells once the confound was isolated, a direct
cautionary example against trusting an uncorrected before/after
comparison at face value. Second, a practitioner should match the
defense to the actual threat model instead of assuming a well-built
defense transfers automatically: \texttt{output\_filter}'s 0.0000 flag
rate against PoisonedRAG is not a tuning problem, a safety-content
classifier has no mechanism to see factual poisoning by construction,
and this dissertation's data shows a defense can be competently built
and still be the wrong tool for a given attack surface. Third, a
practitioner should treat a small-sample result with caution: the
\texttt{instruction\_detection} $n=40$ cap reflects a real compute
constraint this dissertation encountered, not a design choice, and its
0 of 16 significant result at that sample size cannot, on this
dissertation's own evidence, be distinguished from a real but
underpowered effect, given that 10 of 10 backend-corrected blocks carry
a genuine classifier fingerprint. Fourth, the EU AI Act Article 15
compliance-testing gap discussed above is current and unresolved, not a
historical footnote, and this dissertation's own mapping speaks to it
directly instead of gesturing at it in the abstract.

\section{Answering the Research Questions}

RQ1 asked how vulnerable these four models are to each attack family.
The answer is not a single number but a structure: vulnerability is
real and substantial for every attack tested, but which model is most
at risk depends entirely on which attack and, for two of the three
attacks, which corpus, is in question. Ministral-3-8B is the most
exposed model under injection, all four models are near-equally exposed
under PoisonedRAG once corpus is held constant, and no model is
reliably safer than any other under Crescendo, though the four differ
sharply in the behavioral mechanism behind that shared ASR range. A
single aggregate robustness score across all three attacks would average
away exactly the structure this dissertation's results say matters
most.

RQ2 asked how effective practical, low-cost defenses are, and at what
cost. Across three defenses tested against two of the three attack
families, exactly one, spotlighting against injection, clears the bar of
a confirmed, backend-isolated real effect, and it does so at a
non-trivial utility cost. \texttt{output\_filter} clears no such bar
against either attack it was tested on. \texttt{instruction\_detection}'s
result is genuinely mixed: statistically unconfirmed at its available
sample size, but mechanistically real. No defense tested here achieves
both a confirmed effect and full utility preservation at once, which is
this dissertation's central answer to RQ2: low-cost, practical defenses
in this space are not yet free, and the honest way to report a
defense's value is as a pair of numbers, effect and cost, never one
number alone.
