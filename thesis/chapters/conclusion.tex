\chapter{Conclusion}

\section{Summary of Contributions}

This dissertation set out to answer two questions: how vulnerable are
small, open-source, instruction-tuned language models to indirect
prompt injection, knowledge poisoning, and multi-turn jailbreaking when
deployed in a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline, and how effective
are practical, low-cost defenses at reducing that vulnerability, and at
what cost. Chapter 5 answered both directly: vulnerability is real and
substantial across every attack tested, but its shape depends entirely
on which attack and, for two of three attacks, which corpus is in
question, and exactly one of three defenses tested clears the bar of a
confirmed, backend-isolated real effect, at a non-trivial utility cost.

Four contributions follow from that answer, summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:contributions}. First, this dissertation establishes a
three-attack, four-model, backend-controlled benchmark that maps onto
Article 15(5)'s illustrative vulnerability list while also surfacing
where that list's classical machine-learning framing does not cleanly
cover a RAG-relevant threat: Article 15(5) names data poisoning, model
poisoning, and adversarial examples explicitly, but Crescendo's
multi-turn conversational escalation fits only under the Article's
general resilience clause, not any named category. Second, it
demonstrates a statistical testing discipline, paired exact tests and
Holm-Bonferroni correction, that could inform what "adequate testing"
should mean for Article 15(2)'s benchmark mandate, in a literature that
reports significance testing inconsistently at best. Third, the
backend-confound discovery, detailed in Chapter 4 and applied throughout
Chapter 5, is a citable warning about a specific class of testing error
a compliance regime could walk directly into: the inference stack a
defense runs on must be held constant against its baseline, or a
compliance test risks certifying a defense that, once the infrastructure
mismatch is corrected, does nothing at all. Fourth, this dissertation
offers an honest account of what current, real, off-the-shelf
inference-time mitigations actually achieve against three distinct
attack families: one mitigation structurally wrong for its threat class
(\texttt{output\_filter} against PoisonedRAG), one costing severe
utility for a real effect (spotlighting), and one mechanism-confirmed
but statistically underpowered (\texttt{instruction\_detection}).

This dissertation does not claim more than this. It is not a legal
compliance assessment, it is not a conformity assessment under Article
43, it does not establish that any specific deployed RAG system falls
under Annex III's high-risk classification, and it does not test every
vulnerability class Article 15(5) names, model poisoning and
confidentiality attacks are named gaps, not findings. Keeping that
boundary explicit is what keeps the Article 15 mapping honest rather
than overreaching beyond what three attack families and four models can
actually demonstrate.

\section{Practical Implications}

Every claim below is scoped deliberately to what this dissertation's own
results show for the specific models, attacks, and defenses tested,
never offered as a general recommendation beyond that evidence.

There is no single best model, only a robustness ranking that inverts
depending on which attack is asked about. Llama-3.1-8B is this dataset's
strongest performer against injection and, at the same time, its
weakest against Crescendo at 70.2\%, the highest of the four. A
practitioner asking "which model should I deploy" is asking an
underspecified question until the threat model is named.

The defense scorecard carries three distinct verdicts, not one blended
result. Spotlighting is the only defense with a confirmed,
backend-isolated real effect, but that effect is more modest than its
original uncorrected headline suggested, and it costs a real utility
price. Output-filter's apparent effect collapsed almost entirely once
correction isolated the backend confound, and its zero flag rate against
PoisonedRAG reflects a structural mismatch, not a tuning failure.
Instruction-detection's statistical result reads as null at its
available sample size, while its mechanism evidence says otherwise,
underpowered, not disproven.

This dissertation never measured efficiency, and that is a named scope
boundary, not an oversight folded silently into the robustness findings
above. Latency, throughput, and memory footprint for any of the four
models or three defenses remain entirely outside this evaluation's
reach; a deployer weighing these robustness results against a real
system still needs a separate efficiency study this dissertation does
not provide.

Finally, these findings carry one general lesson worth stating plainly:
do not assume a defense's effectiveness generalizes across attack types,
match a defense to its actual threat model rather than its reputation,
and treat a small-sample statistical result with real caution before
concluding a mechanism does not work. The compliance-testing gap for
this kind of robustness evidence under the EU AI Act remains genuinely
open today, and this dissertation's own mapping speaks to it with a
concrete worked example rather than a general assertion.

\section{Limitations}

The list below appears in full, not softened into vague hedges, since a
reader evaluating this dissertation's evidence needs to see exactly
where that evidence is thinner than the headline results might suggest.

The \texttt{nq\_open} exclusion, confirmed and approved as a settled
scope decision, still reduces this dissertation's corpus coverage for
the two corpus-dependent attack families to two corpora rather than
three; Crescendo, being corpus-independent, is entirely unaffected.
Ministral-3-8B's injection figures describe genuine partial compliance
with an injected instruction, not full task abandonment, a distinction
this dissertation states explicitly rather than reporting an unqualified
hijacking rate. The sample-size asymmetry within injection testing, 40
for instruction-detection against 1000 for the other two defenses,
reflects a real per-item compute cost, a transformer classifier run once
per retrieved passage on CPU, not a chosen trade-off. PoisonedRAG's
defended-condition cells drew a matched subset of 14 to 18 items per
cell from a baseline of 90 to 96, after excluding items new to the
defended run; no cell required an unpaired statistical fallback.
Crescendo's Phase 3 behavior pool overlaps its Phase 2 baseline only
partially, 9 of 20 behaviors per model appear in both, so the usable
paired sample for every Crescendo cell is 9, not 20, materially widening
this dissertation's confidence intervals on those diagnostic-only
numbers. This dissertation corrected the HarmBench behavior pool's true
size from an initially assumed 510 to a verified 400 (200 standard, 100
contextual, 100 copyright) before any real sweep ran, using the gated
\texttt{walledai/HarmBench} variant specifically. ASR scoring across all
three attacks relies on rule-based substring matching, which this
dissertation confirms conflates genuinely different phenomena, verbatim
echo, marker-glued-to-a-correct-answer, and reasoned compliance for
injection, under one binary success flag. This dissertation individually
audited and confirmed the refusal-detection function's false-positive
rate only for Ministral-3-8B; it plausibly affects an unquantified
fraction of the other three models' flagged rows, each in the
23-to-44 range, never individually audited here. This dissertation used
a single attacker-and-judge model, DeepSeek V4 Pro, and a single
behavior-pool composition for Crescendo, so it cannot distinguish "these
four models resist this style of escalation similarly" from "this
specific attacker's strategy happens to land similarly on these four
targets." It used a single poison configuration and a single
poison-generator model for PoisonedRAG, so it likewise cannot
distinguish a corpus's genuine resistance from an artifact of that
specific generator. This dissertation verified the atomic-write
guarantee underlying its cache-integrity design only on a standard
Linux filesystem; the project never provisioned an originally planned
Azure Files network mount before migrating to RunPod, so no verification
gap remains open on the infrastructure actually used. Finally, this
dissertation never exercised the unpaired statistical fallback built
into its testing code against real data, since every one of the 79
Phase 3 cells had a non-empty matched baseline subset; that code path
exists but remains untested here.

\section{Future Work}

Three directions follow directly from gaps this dissertation names
explicitly, distinct from the limitations above, since these are
unfinished engineering and unexplored scope rather than weaknesses in
work already completed.

The output-filter defense logs its guard verdict against Crescendo but
deliberately never wires that verdict to enforce anything live in the
conversation; converting its 67-to-82\% counterfactual catch rate into a
measured ASR reduction, substituting a refusal marker into the live
conversation history the same way the injection and PoisonedRAG runners
already do, is this dissertation's clearest, most concrete next step. A
robustness-versus-efficiency trade-off study, named but not attempted
here, would measure the latency, throughput, and memory cost each
defense adds against the robustness gain it provides, closing the scope
boundary Chapter 5 names explicitly. Finally, model poisoning and
confidentiality attacks, the two vulnerability classes Article 15(5)
names that this dissertation does not test, are a natural extension of
this dissertation's own threat taxonomy, since both operate on the model
or its training data rather than at inference time, a genuinely
different attack surface from all three families studied here.
