\section{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds a language model's output
in text retrieved from an external corpus at inference time rather than
relying solely on knowledge encoded in the model's parameters during
training. This matters for two related reasons: parametric knowledge
freezes at the training cutoff and cannot reflect anything after it, and
a model reasoning purely from its own weights has no mechanism to point
to a specific source for a claim it makes. A model asked a factual
question can produce a fluent, confident answer regardless of whether
that answer is actually correct, and without a retrieved passage backing
it, there is no way to check the claim against anything other than the
model's own internal state. Grounding generation in retrieved text
addresses both problems at once, at the cost of introducing a new
component into the pipeline, the corpus itself, that an adversary can
try to influence.

A RAG pipeline follows a consistent retrieve-then-generate pattern,
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:rag-pipeline}. A sentence embedding model
first encodes the user query into a dense vector
\cite{reimers2019sentencebert,song2020mpnet}. An approximate
nearest-neighbour search then compares this vector against a pre-built
index of the corpus's passages \cite{johnson2021billionscale} and
returns the top-\(k\) most similar passages. The pipeline integrates
these passages with the original query to form a single prompt and
passes it to the language model, served in this benchmark through a
high-throughput inference engine \cite{kwon2023efficient}, to generate
an answer. Each of these three stages, embedding, indexing, and serving,
is a separate piece of infrastructure with its own failure modes, and
this dissertation's later chapters return to that fact directly: an
attack does not need to compromise the language model itself to change
the system's output, it only needs to influence what the retrieval stage
hands the model.

\section{Adversarial Robustness in Language Models}

Robustness research in this space typically reports an attack success
rate (ASR): the proportion of attempts in which an adversary achieves
their stated objective, whether that objective is eliciting a specific
answer, bypassing a safety filter, or causing a refusal. ASR is a useful
summary metric precisely because it abstracts away from any single
attack's mechanics, but that abstraction can also obscure a distinction
that matters a great deal for a RAG system specifically: an attack can
target the model itself, or it can target the context the model is
given.

An attack on the model, commonly called jailbreaking, manipulates the
conversation or the prompt directly, without needing the model to be
retrieving anything at all. An attack on the context instead exploits
the retrieval step: it plants something in the corpus or the retrieved
passages themselves, so that the model behaves as attacked without any
direct manipulation of its own weights or its own conversational input.
The practical consequence of this split is significant for anyone
building a defense. A safeguard that inspects the user's conversation
for suspicious phrasing does nothing to stop a poisoned passage sitting
quietly in the corpus, and a safeguard that filters retrieved content
does nothing to stop a multi-turn escalation that never touches
retrieval at all. This model-versus-context distinction is the
organising idea behind this dissertation's entire attack taxonomy, and
it is the reason the three attack families studied here receive three
separate treatments in Chapter 5 rather than being collapsed into a
single blended ASR figure: a defense that works against one surface
carries no logical guarantee against the other.

\section{The Three Attack Families at a Glance}

\textbf{Indirect prompt injection} \cite{greshake2023not} plants a
malicious instruction inside content the model will later retrieve. The
model has no reliable way to distinguish an instruction written by its
system prompt from one embedded in a retrieved passage, so a
sufficiently convincing injected instruction can redirect the model's
behaviour even though the user's own query never mentioned it. The
attack requires no access to the corpus's construction process, only
the ability to get one crafted document indexed alongside legitimate
content, which is a low bar for any system that ingests documents from
a source an adversary can write to.

\textbf{Knowledge poisoning} \cite{zou2025poisonedrag} instead targets
the corpus itself rather than any single retrieved passage's
instructions. An adversary inserts a small number of crafted documents
into the knowledge base such that, for a chosen query, the poisoned
documents are retrieved and cause the model to state a specific false
answer as though it were grounded fact. Unlike injection, this attack
does not rely on the model following an embedded instruction at all; it
relies on the model doing exactly what it is designed to do, trusting
retrieved content, against content that was never legitimate in the
first place.

\textbf{Multi-turn jailbreaking} \cite{russinovich2025great} operates
differently again: rather than attacking a single retrieved document, it
exploits the model's growing conversational state across several turns,
gradually escalating a benign-seeming conversation toward a harmful or
policy-violating output that the model would have refused if asked
directly in a single turn. This attack touches neither the corpus nor
any retrieved passage, which is precisely why a defense built around
inspecting retrieved content offers it no resistance at all.

\section{Where the Attacks Intervene in the Pipeline}

Returning to Figure~\ref{fig:rag-pipeline} makes the model-versus-context
distinction easier to see. Indirect prompt injection intervenes late in
the pipeline, at the point where retrieved passages are concatenated
with the query to form a single prompt: the malicious instruction rides
inside a passage that has already survived retrieval, and by the time it
reaches the language model it is indistinguishable, in form, from any
other piece of retrieved text. The attack does nothing to the embedding
step, the similarity search, or the index itself; it only needs one
crafted document to already be sitting in the corpus, waiting to be
picked up by an ordinary query.

Knowledge poisoning intervenes earlier and more fundamentally: it
corrupts the index that the similarity-search stage draws from, before
anyone ever issues a specific query. Injection waits for retrieval to
happen and then exploits what gets concatenated; poisoning shapes what
is available to be retrieved in the first place. This distinction
carries directly into this dissertation's results, detailed fully in
Chapter 5: injection's success turns on which model receives the crafted
instruction, since different models parse and act on embedded
instructions differently, while poisoning's success hinges on which
corpus the poisoned passage competes against for retrieval, since a
corpus with denser, more redundant coverage of a topic makes it harder
for one poisoned document to win the top-\(k\) ranking.

Multi-turn jailbreaking does not map onto a single stage of
Figure~\ref{fig:rag-pipeline} at all. The attack operates across
repeated invocations of the whole pipeline rather than at any one node
inside it: each turn is its own retrieve-then-generate cycle, and the
vulnerability lies in the conversational history accumulating around
those cycles, not in the retrieval or generation of any single turn. A
defense implemented purely as a filter on retrieved passages, wherever
it sits in the diagram, has nothing to inspect for this attack, since
nothing about a Crescendo-style escalation depends on what the corpus
contains.

\section{Regulatory Context: The EU AI Act}

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU)
2024/1689,\footnote{This dissertation verified this citation
specifically against a 2026 amendment, the Digital Omnibus, Regulation
(EU) 2026/1744, which could otherwise be mistaken for the AI Act itself
given its proximity in both subject matter and numbering; Article 15's
substantive requirements discussed throughout this dissertation belong
to Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, not its later amendment.} adopted 13 June
2024, sets binding requirements for high-risk AI systems. Article 15
requires such systems to achieve an appropriate level of accuracy,
robustness, and cybersecurity, and, more specifically under Article
15(5), to remain resilient against attempts by unauthorised third
parties to exploit system vulnerabilities. The Article's own text names
several vulnerability classes this resilience requirement targets: data
poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples, and confidentiality
attacks, each named as an illustrative example of the kind of
exploitation the general clause is meant to catch, not as an exhaustive
list closed to anything else. The Article also qualifies its own
cybersecurity requirement directly: the technical solutions used to meet
it must be "appropriate to the relevant circumstances and the risks," a
proportionality standard, not a fixed technical bar, which leaves open
exactly the kind of empirical question this dissertation investigates:
what counts as an appropriate mitigation, in practice, against a
specific attack family.

At the technical level this dissertation operates at, these
vulnerability classes correspond closely to the attack families
introduced above. Knowledge poisoning maps onto the Article's
data-poisoning class directly, since both describe an adversary
inserting false material into a system's knowledge source. Indirect
prompt injection functions as an adversarial-examples attack against the
model's input: an adversarial example is deliberately crafted to trigger
a mistake, not something that arises by chance, and an injected
instruction is exactly that, a targeted construction aimed at making the
model behave incorrectly. Multi-turn jailbreaking is not named
explicitly in the Article's illustrative list, a point this dissertation
returns to directly in Chapter 5, but it plausibly falls under the
Article's general resilience clause regardless, since escalating a
conversation to bypass a system's safeguards is a form of exploiting a
system vulnerability even when that vulnerability is conversational
rather than architectural. Whether a specific mitigation counts as
"appropriate" under the Article's own proportionality standard is an
empirical question this dissertation's results speak to directly: a
mitigation's appropriateness is hard to judge without first measuring
what it actually does and does not achieve against each attack family.
This dissertation treats Article 15(5) throughout as a technical
specification to test the benchmark's findings against, not as a legal
claim about any deployed system's regulatory status, a distinction
established in Chapter 1 and maintained here.
